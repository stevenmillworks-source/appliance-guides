LG Dryer Not Heating

Quick answer: Start with the free checks: heat-setting selected, both breakers live, and a clear exhaust vent. If those pass, the thermal fuse is the most common electrical failure, followed by the heating element — both are multimeter continuity tests.

Safety first

  • Unplug the appliance before any inspection that goes beyond the exterior — capacitors and live terminals can hold a charge even when powered off.
  • Never bypass a safety device (thermal fuse, door switch, interlock). It opened for a reason; bypassing it creates a fire or shock hazard.
  • If you smell burning, see arcing, or water is contacting electrical parts, stop and call a professional.

Diagnosis path (in order)

  1. Confirm the cycle uses heat. Air-dry/no-heat options run cold on purpose. Also confirm a drying-sensor cycle did not end early reading dry clothes.
  2. Check both sides of the breaker. Electric dryers use a 240 V circuit: one tripped half leaves the drum turning with no heat. Reset firmly at the panel.
  3. Clean the full exhaust path. A blocked vent is both a fire risk and the top cause of heat cutoffs. Disconnect the hose at the dryer, vacuum the run, and confirm the outdoor hood flaps freely. If the laundry room is humid and loads take multiple cycles, a restriction is almost certain.
  4. Test the thermal fuse (power off). The thermal fuse is a one-shot safety on the exhaust path. Pull one wire, set the meter to continuity: a beep means good, silence means blown. A blown fuse almost always has a cause — find the restriction (previous step) before replacing or it will blow again [EST $10–20 part].
  5. Test the operating and high-limit thermostats. Both should show continuity at room temperature. An open high-limit reinforces the venting/overheating story.
  6. Test the heating element. On most LG models the element sits behind the drum. Measure across its terminals: an open circuit (no continuity) confirms a failed element [EST $30–80 part].
  7. Gas models diverge here. For gas dryers the sequence checks the igniter first, then the flame sensor, then the gas valve coils — a glowing igniter that never lights usually means coils that fail when hot.
  8. Last suspects. If every thermal device and the element pass, the moisture-sensor bars and the main control board remain — rare, and worth a wiring-diagram session or a technician before spending.

Tools you may need

Screwdrivers (Phillips and flat), a flashlight, and towels for water work. Electrical checks need a basic multimeter (continuity mode) — a $15–25 household model is sufficient [EST].

Model note [SYM]: Electric vs gas changes the diagnosis path completely — confirm your fuel type and model number inside the door opening before ordering parts.

Typical diagnosis time: 45–90 minutes including vent cleaning [EST]

Frequently asked questions

Is it worth repairing or should I replace?

Rule of thumb: repair when the fix costs under about half of replacement and the appliance is under 8–10 years old [EST]. Confirm your exact model number first — it changes both the parts and the diagnosis.

Do I need a multimeter?

For any electrical check (fuses, elements, sensors, switches) yes. A basic continuity-capable meter is the single most useful appliance-repair tool [EST $15–25].

Where is my model number?

Washers/dryers: inside the door or lid rim. Refrigerators: inside a compartment wall. Ovens: behind the drawer or inside the door. Dishwashers: door rim. Record the full number before ordering parts.
